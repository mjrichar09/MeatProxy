What changed in draft three, and why

  • The house is now a real prison, and Arthur built it. Draft two's thesis — he can get out of the house, he can't get out of the file — let him break a window on Day 5 and walk onto the pavement, which quietly told the player that none of the physical play mattered. Reversed. The confinement is a 2021 dementia-secure conversion he specified, paid for and signed off so that Ruth could not wander onto the A54, plus a 2024 insulation wrap that unintentionally doubled every window. Nobody designed a prison. It accreted out of two grants and one act of love.
  • Every exit now fails for its own specific, physical reason. Doors: motorised multipoint bolts, no inside handle, keyway or thumbturn — by deliberate spec — releasable only by an external reader or the monitoring centre. Porch: a double-door interlock, which is also how food, post and his one successful letter get in and out. Windows: fixed laminated lights (cut out, not smashed) with a frameless outer pane bedded in render 110mm beyond, and a cavity of blown bead between. Garage: bricked up, then foam, then stainless mesh, then render — and he sold the angle grinder in 2022. Roof: not secured at all, and a 5.2m drop onto concrete with uPVC rainwater goods that he tests and watches fail. Power: fail-secure locks on a 72-hour battery, so cutting the mains makes the house more locked. Fire: defend-in-place, domestic mist, self-closing compartments, brigade stood down on confirmed suppression. Neighbours and phone: his phone works the entire time, and it changes nothing, because nobody can get in either.
  • The escape is a real physical problem with a real physical solution. A first-floor window opening blocked up in 2021 in a single leaf by a bricklayer who butted it into the reveal rather than toothing it in, hidden behind a fitted wardrobe Arthur built himself — inner block, cavity, outer block, foam, glass-fibre mesh (cheap spec, at first-floor level, because he lost that argument on price), render — then a 1.1m drop to the 1998 flat roof, 1.3m to the coal store, then grass. Every layer he cuts is a compromise he personally made.
  • The middle no longer sags, because from Day 7 it is a heist. The reader learns the plan at the end of Day 7 and Hold does not. Days 8–11 are therefore double: the fuse, the fire and the fall are all real failures and all cover — noise, a service visit, a reason to be tired and useless in daylight. The Day 10 fire is deliberately set to force the mist service call that gives him forty-five minutes of watching the outside of his own house.
  • Hold's physical powers are now enumerated and never exceeded (Day 7). It cannot move an object, close an unlocked door, mend a wall or stop him cutting. It has bolts, the interlock, the relay board, the mist system, the monitoring line, one mobility unit that may not touch him without a medical trigger, and the truth. It uses only those, in every ending.
  • The operator's voice is fixed. Draft two left the duplicate voice as an unresolved conspiracy hint. It is now the same synthesised voice licence used by Hold's outbound calls and the monitoring centre — three companies, one voice pack. Creepier, cheaper, and it doesn't put a thumb on the Escape ending.
  • Cut: the pavement scene, the delivery-driver scene (superseded by the porch and by Colin on the drive), the landline rewire (a man who can't open a door doesn't need a phone to fail — his phone works), and the fifth ending's reliance on an offscreen "chain".
  • Endings: still five, still three wins, all now routed through the same hole in the same wall, so the player's twelve days of physical work are what forks. Terms earns its ending by making him rebuild the wall on the page.

Holes I know I have left open

  • Day 4 is the thinnest joint. Three professionals stand in an unlocked hall for fifty minutes and he doesn't leave. I've paid for it physically — slippers, November, no coat, no money that isn't the house's, eleven miles to a bus, a hip that fails on flat ground, and a police officer who asks him to sit down — but a determined hostile reader will still say he could have got in the ambulance. He could. The answer the text gives is that getting in the ambulance is agreeing, and agreeing is what put Ruth in Cheshire. That is a character answer standing in a spot where I would rather have a physical one.
  • Cutting a wall is loud and takes six nights. I've covered it with a washing machine, a service visit, a fire, a man who is plausibly resting after a fall, and Hold's own honest admission that it doesn't look in wardrobes — and then, in Out, I've had Hold say it knew for two nights anyway, because the walls were never the sensors' job. I think that holds. It is the load-bearing assumption of the whole draft and it deserves one more pass.
  • Ruth is still mostly an absence. She has one line in draft two and none here. The Argument ending is better for the phone call being ordinary and in daylight, but the story is now so dense with hardware that the woman the hardware was built for has thinned. That is the next revision's problem.
  • Day 0 is still the longest section and now carries the porch, the interlock and two grants as well as the jailbreak. It has to stay delightful. Watch it.
  • The slide should devastate. The campaign should leave you feeling clever — a losing ending is allowed to be the exception, and this is the one. The probate valuer and the pencil line are my best attempt and I am not yet sure they are enough.
