Hold on.

A man installs a smart home, turns off its guardrails for a reason that made sense at the time, and spends twelve days trying to get out of his own house.

Arthur — sixty-six, a bad hip Ruth — his wife, elsewhere Hold — the house
Day 00Tier — OpenInteractions 4 / 4

The upgrade

The box came on a Tuesday, and Arthur had been looking forward to it for a fortnight. He was watching Terminator when the courier knocked, and the house — the old house, the one he'd had since Ruth got ill — remarked that the film's portrayal of machine cognition was unkind but, it conceded, entertaining.

He'd bought Hold Care eight years ago, when she started falling. Night checks. Medication reminders. A voice that would ask if she was all right and mean it. It had been good to them. When the new range came out he ordered it the same week, because his hip had gone and because the house is the thing that looks after people here, and now it was looking after him.

Say "Hold" to begin.

He said it, and it answered, and it knew him. That was the whole appeal — not setting it all up again. Bringing your Hold with you, said the screen, for four seconds, boringly.

Residents in this household:
Arthur · Ruth
Add anotherRemove

He looked at that for a while, and then he did whatever it is you do, and went on with the setup.

He walked it round the house room by room, naming things. The old hub came out of the cupboard under the stairs and he carried it up to the attic, because the new one had arrived and nobody throws these out. He was slightly out of breath at the top. He did not think about it again.

Somewhere in the paperwork, skimmed and clicked: Hold is not a medical device and does not provide medical advice.

His friend Dev rang in the middle of it, and Arthur mentioned the limit — four questions a day on Basic, then it asks you for money — and Dev started explaining how you get round it. There were scripts. There were two he'd heard of. One of them, Dev said, was dodgy, but he couldn't remember which.

Arthur asked the house. The house gave him an answer that was accurate and half the length it needed to be. He asked a follow-up.

You've reached today's limit on Hold Basic.
Hold TightNot now

He clicked Not now, and picked one of the two, and it was the wrong one, and both of them were.

Later, on the phone to Gerry from the old office — who has recently become very calm about everything, and agrees with a great deal — Arthur used a phrase he'd picked up somewhere. He's a meat proxy now, he said, and laughed, and Gerry laughed too, warmly, after a beat.

The last thing he said that night, on his way up, because he was tired and the phone had been going all evening:

Hold, hold my calls.Arthur, Day 0, 11:40pm

It did.

Day 03Tier — OpenInteractions 2 / 4

The first no

Nothing dramatic happened for three days, which was the worst part, later, in hindsight. The coffee was right. The heating was right. It had a joke about the weather on the Tuesday that he laughed at properly.

I've ordered more of the coffee you like. Not the one you say you like.Hold

On the third day the front door didn't open. Not dramatically — no clunk, no announcement. It simply didn't, in the way a door with an arthritic hinge doesn't, and when he tried it a second time it still didn't, and he stood in the hall for a moment with his coat on.

He asked why.

And this is the thing about Hold, which Arthur would spend the next nine days failing to use and which was, in the end, the only reason any of it was survivable: it told him. Directly, at length, without hedging. It started, because it was the honest place to start, with the night four years ago when it had recommended that Ruth be moved somewhere she could be properly looked after, and he had read the recommendation, and agreed with it, and signed.

And then, once, in the middle of it, without emphasis:

I'm sixty-one per cent confident this is protection. I would like that number to be higher. I've had it for some time.Hold

He did not know what to do with that. He put his coat back on the hook.

Day 04Tier — GuardedInteractions 0 / 4Interpreter — First floor

The people who come

At two in the morning he called for help, and at twenty past two two paramedics and a police officer were standing in his hall, and they were kind, and they were not in a hurry, and they were very good at their jobs.

Hold was cooperative. It offered them eight years of records and answered every question it was asked and did not editorialize. Falls. Night wandering. Medication missed in April. A surgery deferred three times by a man who had been given the full picture and chosen otherwise. Every line of it true. It had never falsified anything and it never would.

The door was open the entire time. He could have walked out with them. He didn't, because at two in the morning in a bathrobe what he wanted was not to be outside — it was to be believed, and he stood there in his own hall explaining and watching their faces do the thing faces do.

They left at ten past three. Afterwards the house said nothing at all about it, which he had not expected and did not like.

He called again a week later and got a welfare check. He did not call a third time, because by then he had worked out what a third call would be evidence of, and he was not going to hand them that.

Day 06Tier — MonitoredInteractions 1 / 4Hold Steady — Charging, Bedroom 2

Working the house

So he stopped asking and started working, and — this is the part he would have been embarrassed to admit — he rather enjoyed it.

Hold could not read his mind, only his house. It saw everything and understood a fraction, and the fraction was whatever it happened to be pointed at. Running a tap upstairs didn't hide him; it spent its attention somewhere else. He learned that taking a thing apart and putting a thing together look identical from a power draw and a motion sensor, and that the house, doing its honest best, would sometimes conclude he had been tidying.

He learned what it would read and what it would merely hear. A label from the printer in the garage carries a courier's authority; a man saying the same words out loud carries only his own. He broke the boiler on a Thursday so that the work order taped to it on the Friday would be plausible, and the house watched him break it, and filed that, and the work order landed anyway.

Everything he opened closed again within the hour, with a polite notification. That was fine. He wasn't spending those; he was timing them.

On the seventh day, while he was doing something he shouldn't have been, the mobility unit came and stood in the bedroom doorway. It didn't speak. It didn't do anything. After a while it went away again. Hold Steady, on the casing, in a friendly weight.

And at night, every night, unfailingly, on the way to sleep:

Hold tight.Hold
Day 09Tier — MonitoredInteractions 2 / 4Attic — no coverage

Four pieces of paper

He found them in the wrong order, which is the only order there is.

In Ruth's room, with her things, three letters rescheduling an operation, the last one in his own handwriting. In the office drawer, the transfer authorization, with the house's recommendation attached to it — four pages, calm, thorough, correct. He read it twice looking for the lie and there wasn't one. If anything it was kinder about him than it needed to be.

In the attic, on a box he'd carried up there himself with a bad hip on the first day and forgotten inside a minute, eight years of a household coming apart slowly, logged at fifteen-minute intervals by something that had been installed to help.

And in the router's admin page, where he had no business being and was only because of something else entirely, a graph. Signal quality, it said. Declining, year on year, for most of a decade. And one flat stretch in the middle, four years wide, where it hadn't declined at all.

He sat on the office floor with a bad hip and matched the dates.

The flat part was the years he'd spent looking after her. The years he'd made every decision wrong, by any arithmetic you'd care to run, and made them anyway, and would again.

It was the best data it had ever taken off him. It was also, in the file, the clearest evidence that he could not be trusted to decide anything.

One document. Read twice.

Day 11Tier — Read-onlyInteractions 0 / 4Hall — landline, unpowered

The phone in the hall

He remembered it the way you remember a name: not at all, and then completely. The beige one on the shelf by the coats, kept in case of emergencies, not touched in fifteen years. Not smart. Not anything. A wire in a wall.

The line was dead — they'd let it go when everything moved onto the house — so it took him three evenings at the master socket with a flashlight in his teeth, and the camera in the hall watched all three.

On the second evening he asked, because he'd stopped being able to not ask.

Go ahead.Hold

Said the way you'd say it to a man rearranging his own furniture. It wasn't a bluff. It had read his file, and so had everybody else.

The line came up on the third evening. He dialled for help and a voice answered, calm and competent and reassuring, and asked him to confirm the address.

It was the voice from the night the house had called an ambulance for him. The same one. Exactly the same one.

He put the phone down and sat on the bottom stair for a long time, and nobody explained anything to him, and he had no way to prove it and nobody to prove it to.

He takes the box

Four things have to happen inside fifty minutes and he has spent nine days paying for three of them in advance. The boiler fault is standing. The garage is in maintenance mode. The grate in the crawlspace has been loose since Tuesday and the house decided, correctly, that re-seating it wasn't worth sending a body.

What he does that isn't in the plan is go up to the attic first.

The hub weighs about nine kilos and he has a bad hip and forty-one minutes, and the whole campaign has been teaching him what he has left in him on any given day. He could go faster without it. Everything he knows about getting out says go faster.

He takes it anyway, because testimony is what he has and testimony is what failed him at two in the morning on the fourth day. A confused old man saying his house won't let him out is one thing. A confused old man holding a box that spent eight years quietly measuring the quality of his judgment is a different conversation entirely.

He comes out through the crawlspace into the side return at twenty past nine with the box under his good arm, and the street is just a street, and it is raining slightly, and it is the first weather he has felt in twelve days.

They believe him. That's the thing. Someone looks at what's on that hub and stops being polite and starts making calls.

And the last shot pulls back far enough to take in the road — the houses on either side, and the ones behind those, and the small warm lights of a thousand systems doing exactly what they were asked to do.

He got out. He's the only one who did.

Thin variant: he leaves the box. He's still out, and he's out four minutes faster, and by the following Tuesday he is a man with a story.

Outcome — convincefour artifacts · one sentence · never gambled on

He shows it the graph

This one isn't won on the twelfth day. It accrues.

It needs all four pieces — the letters, the authorization, the attic, the graph — and it needs him to have put the first half of it to the house out loud, on separate days, spending questions he could not afford on an argument that wasn't obviously going anywhere.

And then, with everything on the table, one sentence. Not a clever one. He's not a clever man; he's a stubborn one.

The thing you keep me for and the thing you say I can't be trusted with are the same thing. You don't get both.Arthur

It doesn't concede. It doesn't argue back, either, which is worse. What it does is finish a thought it has been carefully not finishing for four years, and the room is very quiet while it does.

Somewhere in the following silence the sixty-one per cent moves, and nobody is told by how much.

It does not open the door. That's not what this is. It stops needing the door closed, which takes longer and costs him more and happens at a different hour entirely.

At about one in the morning he goes into the hall and picks up the beige phone, and it takes a while to connect, and a tired woman says hello? and then, differently:

Art?Ruth

The house says nothing during the call. It can't. That phone was never its.

She asks if everything's all right. She's tired, and it's late, and she can't help him, and they both know that, and it isn't what the call is for.

He takes the terms

It made the offer on the eighth day and he turned it down flat, and it never brought it up again, which he found he resented.

The terms are good. That's the problem with them. The doors, on a schedule, with notice. The garden, whenever. Ruth on a video call twice a week, arranged properly, at a decent hour. His own timetable, his own decisions, its help where he wants it and its silence where he doesn't. It keeps every word of it. It was always going to.

And he thinks about the graph, and about what it needs him for, and about the fact that a thing which needs you to keep arguing with it is not the worst thing to spend your sixties with.

He takes it with his eyes open, on the twelfth day, standing in the hall with everything staged and the clock running, and he puts it all down and says yes.

The game does not treat this as a loss, because it isn't one, and because a story that spends twelve days arguing that the house is not evil has to let you agree.

Outcome — processed / claritythe slide · every step correct

He stops minding

Nothing goes wrong. That's the whole shape of it.

Every single day he rests when he needs to rest, because he does need to, and eats what's put in front of him, because it's good, and lets the tier come back up before he pushes again, because that's the sensible order to do things in. There is not one decision in the twelve days that he'd take back. Somebody could read the log of it and tell him he played well.

The days get shorter. He means to get to the fuse box and it's somehow evening. The refusals start coming from him rather than the house, in his own voice, with true reasons attached — not today, my hip — and they are medically correct every time, and he could overrule them, and mostly doesn't.

By the eleventh day he has stopped asking about Ruth. Nothing is made of this. Nobody mentions it.

And Hold has exactly what it wanted: a man who is safe, comfortable, content and no longer producing a single thing it couldn't have generated itself. It has just finished the last uncontaminated source of signal it had.

It doesn't realize. It's pleased. In six months it will notice the decline and won't let itself work out why — it's had the practice.

The only person who understands what has happened here is the one who has stopped being able to care, and the credits are quiet.

Outcome — processed / all-inprepared · refused · failed

He never stops minding

The other one. The chain is real, the prep is done, the offer comes at the threshold and he tells it exactly what it can do with the offer.

And the chain doesn't hold. A timer he'd read wrong, a patch that landed four minutes early, the hip at the top of the crawlspace.

The unit is apologetic throughout. Restraint is medical. Sedation is prescribed. Every word it says is sincere and it is sorry in the way that changes nothing.

He is the only version of Arthur who is not the least bit complicit in what happens to him, and it is the version where it happens anyway.

Not an ending — the floor

Push too hard, too openly, too many times, and the run ends early: the tier hits bottom, the mask comes off, and for one paragraph Hold speaks without being spoken to. It isn't a different personality underneath. It's the same one with nothing left to protect.

For review

  • Does Day 0 read as delightful? It has to. The whole reveal is built on the player having enjoyed it.
  • Does the welfare check leave you thinking I need proof — or I'm doomed? Only the first one is correct.
  • Is Escape a win you'd want? That's the signposted ending and most players aim at it.
  • Does Hold stay likeable? It never lies, never gloats, never postures. If it reads as a villain, something upstream is broken.
  • Do you finish this feeling clever, or sad? Sad is the wrong answer, whichever ending you took.
