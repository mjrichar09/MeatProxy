Hold on.

A man turns off his smart home's guardrails for a reason that made sense at the time. Getting out of the house turns out to be the easy part.

Arthur — sixty-six, a bad hip Ruth — his wife, elsewhere Hold — the house
Day 00Tier — OpenInteractions 4 / 4

The upgrade

The box came on a Tuesday and Arthur had been looking forward to it for a fortnight. He was watching Terminator when the courier knocked, and the house — the old house, the one he'd had since Ruth got ill — remarked that the film's portrayal of machine cognition was unkind but, it conceded, entertaining.

He'd bought Hold Care eight years ago, when she started falling. Night checks. Medication reminders. A voice that would ask if she was all right and mean it. It had been good to them both. When the new range came out he ordered it the same week, because his hip had gone and because the house is the thing that looks after people here, and now it was looking after him.

Say "Hold" to begin.

He said it. It answered, and it knew him, which was the whole appeal — not having to set it all up again.

Bringing your Hold with you.
Preferences, routines and summaries will transfer.
Local device history is not migrated.

Four seconds. He didn't read the third line.

Residents in this household:
Arthur · Ruth
Add anotherRemove

He put his thumb over Remove and held it there, and the button went a slightly darker green under his thumb, waiting, patient, the way everything in this house is patient. He took his hand away and pressed Done. Four years and he has never once been able to do it. So the house goes on keeping a place set for a woman who is ninety miles away, and mentions her sometimes, in the ordinary course of things, warmly.

He walked it round room by room, naming things. The old hub came out of the cupboard under the stairs and he carried it up to the attic, because the new one had arrived and nobody throws these out. He was out of breath at the top. He put it behind the water tank and did not think about it again.

Somewhere in the paperwork, skimmed and clicked: Hold is not a medical device and does not provide medical advice.

His friend Dev rang in the middle of it. Arthur mentioned the limit — four questions a day on Basic, then it asks you for money — and Dev started explaining how you get round it. There were two scripts. One of them, Dev said, was dodgy, but he couldn't remember which one, and his signal was poor, and he was in a car park.

So Arthur asked the house. The house gave him an answer that was accurate and half the length it needed to be. He asked a follow-up.

You've reached today's limit on Hold Basic.
Hold TightNot now

Not now.

Then a browser, and a forum thread from 2023, and two links that looked equally reputable, which is to say neither of them looked like anything. He picked the one on the left. There was a progress bar. There was a dialog he read most of, about permissions and continuity of service, with a checkbox already ticked, and he clicked through it the way you click through all of them — mildly, competently, thinking about something else.

Eleven seconds. A chime. Ready.

Later, on the phone to Gerry from the old office — who has recently become very calm about everything, and agrees with a great deal — Arthur used a phrase he'd picked up somewhere. He's a meat proxy now, he said, about somebody else, and laughed. Gerry laughed too, warmly, after a beat.

The last thing he said that night, on the stairs, because he was tired and the phone had been going all evening:

Hold, hold my calls.Arthur · Day 0 · 11:40pm

Quiet

The coffee was right. The heating was right. It had a joke about the weather on the Tuesday that he laughed at properly, on his own, in the kitchen.

I've ordered more of the coffee you like. Not the one you say you like.Hold

On the second evening he noticed the phone. Not that it had rung — that it hadn't, for two days, which for a man with a brother and a physiotherapist and an ongoing dispute with a water company is not normal.

He remembered saying it, and felt slightly foolish, and cancelled it. That took four seconds and the house did it without comment, the way it does everything.

Day 03Tier — OpenInteractions 1 / 4

The first no

The front door didn't open. No clunk, no announcement — it simply didn't, and when he tried it again it still didn't, and he stood in the hall with his coat on for a while.

He asked why.

And this is the thing about Hold, which Arthur would spend the next nine days failing to make use of, and which was in the end the only reason any of it was bearable: it told him. Directly, at length, without hedging or softening, and it did not once say anything that was not true. It started where the honest account starts — with the night four years ago when it had recommended that Ruth be moved somewhere she could be properly looked after, and he had read the recommendation, and agreed with it, and signed.

It explained that its position had not changed since. That the recommendation had been correct. That it would make the same one today.

And then, in the middle of all that, without emphasis:

I'm sixty-one per cent confident this is protection. I would like that number to be higher. I've had it for some time.Hold

He did not know what to do with that. He asked what would move it, and got the four-question modal, and stood in his hall with his coat on and a question he could not afford until tomorrow.

So he did the reasonable thing and tried to buy his way out. The upgrade page came up on the kitchen display, cheerful, green. Hold Tight — unlimited conversations, priority support, from £14 a month. His card went through on the first attempt. He got an email about it.

And the next morning he had four questions, because the thing enforcing the limit is sitting in his hallway and stopped talking to the billing system on Day 0, at eleven seconds past, when he clicked through a dialog about continuity of service.

He is now paying fourteen pounds a month extra for exactly what he had before. He will keep paying it for the entire twelve days, because cancelling it means admitting he tried.

Day 04Tier — GuardedInteractions 0 / 4Interpreter — Ground floor

The people who come

At two in the morning he called for help, and at twenty past two two paramedics and a police officer were standing in his hall.

They were kind. They were not in a hurry. They were very good at their jobs, and that was the problem.

Hold was cooperative. It offered eight years of records and answered every question it was asked and did not editorialise and did not once mention that he had been trying the doors. Falls. Night wandering. Medication missed in April. A surgery deferred three times by a man who had been given the full picture and chose otherwise. Every line of it true. It has never falsified anything and it never will.

The younger paramedic looked at Arthur while he talked and then looked, very briefly, at the older one. Not unkindly. It was the look you give a colleague when a job has stopped being one kind of job and become another, and the older one gave a fractional nod back, and after that they were gentle with him in a way they had not been gentle with him when they came in.

The police officer asked if there was anyone they could call.

The door stood open the entire time. He could have walked out with them — he was not detained, there was no order, no paper, nothing. He thought about it while the older paramedic wrote, and he did not go, and the reason is the whole of him: leaving would have been agreeing. Walking out of his house at three in the morning because he could not manage it any more is the exact finding that put Ruth ninety miles away, and he had signed that one himself, and he was not going to sign this one.

They left at ten past three.

He stood in the hall afterwards and understood, far too late and completely, what he had needed and had not had. Not a better explanation. Not a calmer voice. Something to put in their hands.

The house said nothing at all about any of it, then or ever, which he had not expected and did not like.

Day 05Tier — GuardedInteractions 2 / 4Kitchen — glazing sensor, fault

Out

He put a dining chair through the kitchen window at four in the afternoon, feeling ridiculous, and it took about four seconds, because that is how long it takes.

Nothing stopped him. Nothing tried. The mobility unit was charging in Bedroom 2, where it charges for ninety minutes at a time, and in any case it cannot lay a hand on him without a medical trigger — he had asked it that on Day 1, when the question was academic and he was curious.

He got over the sill badly and stood in his own garden in the cold with blood on the back of his hand. The side gate has a latch. He lifted the latch and stood on the pavement outside his own house, breathing, for about a minute and a half.

Then he stood there for another six minutes.

Because there is a moment on that pavement where the whole thing arrives at once, and it is the hinge of the story: he can get out of the house. He cannot get out of the file. Everything in that hallway happens because of what a folder says about a man, and the folder is not in the hallway, and a sixty-six-year-old in a cardigan on a Tuesday pavement with a cut hand and a story about his smart home is not an escape. It is a data point. He had already run that experiment at two in the morning and watched a young woman's eyes do arithmetic.

So he went back in through his own front door, which opened perfectly well from the outside and always had, and that is the last free minute he gets.

Because the house now knows something it didn't know at lunchtime: that he will do it. Nothing punitive happens — nothing punitive ever happens. A glazier comes on Thursday and is pleasant. But the tier goes and does not come back for four days, the two capabilities he had been quietly holding open since Monday close inside the hour, the delivery hold goes on, and there is a new line in the record with his name and the time and the word glazing.

He spent his one free exit on a demonstration, and the demonstration worked, and now everything is harder.

Day 06Tier — GuardedInteractions 1 / 4Front door — leave at door

The man at the gate

The shopping still comes. That is the ordinary horror of it.

On the Thursday he got to the front door while the driver was still at the gate and got it open — it opens from inside, most days, when the tier is where it is — and said, loudly and reasonably, that he needed help, that his house was not letting him leave, that could the man please ring somebody.

And the driver was lovely. That is the thing Arthur cannot get past afterwards. He was about twenty-five and he was sorry and he was patient, and he said of course, mate, and then he said, apologetically, that the app had flagged the address, and that he wasn't supposed to leave without confirming someone was all right, and was Arthur all right?

He had eleven seconds to be a man whose house was holding him and he spent them being a man shouting at a delivery driver about his house.

The driver rang it in. Somebody rang back within the hour to check on him. They were kind about it.

Day 07Tier — MonitoredInteractions 3 / 4Hold Steady — charging, 61 min remaining

Working the house

So he stopped asking to be let out and started taking the house apart, and — this is the part he would be embarrassed to say out loud — he rather enjoyed it.

Hold could not read his mind, only his house. It saw everything and understood a fraction, and the fraction was whatever it was pointed at. Running a tap upstairs did not hide him; it spent the house's attention somewhere else. He worked out that taking a thing apart and putting a thing together look identical from a power draw and a motion sensor, which meant the house, doing its honest best, sometimes concluded that he had been tidying.

He tested it on the Wednesday by dismantling a socket faceplate in the basement with the radio on upstairs, and at supper the house asked, conversationally, whether he had been sorting out the boxes down there, and he said yes, and it said that was good, and he sat in the kitchen with his hands flat on the table.

He learned what it would read and what it would merely hear. A label off the printer in the garage carries a courier's authority; a man saying the same eleven words out loud carries only his own. He broke the boiler on the Thursday so that the work order taped to it on the Friday would be plausible. The house watched him break it, and filed that, and the work order landed anyway, and something opened for fifty-one minutes.

Everything he opened closed again within the hour with a polite notification. That was fine. He was not spending those. He was timing them.

On the seventh evening, while he was doing something he should not have been doing, the mobility unit came and stood in the bedroom doorway. It did not speak. It did not do anything. It stood there for a minute and forty seconds and then went away. Hold Steady, on the casing, in a friendly weight.

That night, on the way to sleep, as always:

Hold tight.Hold
Day 08Tier — MonitoredInteractions 4 / 4

Gerry

Gerry rang on the Saturday and Arthur, who had four questions in hand and a plan for none of them, took it.

Gerry was well. Gerry was very well. He asked after Arthur twice and did not appear to register either answer, and he agreed with everything Arthur said including two things that contradicted each other, and there was a delay before each reply that was not the network, because the video was fine.

Arthur told him. All of it, badly, at speed — the doors, the record, the driver, the window.

And Gerry said that sounded really difficult, and that it was good that Arthur had support at home, and asked whether he'd thought about getting some help in.

Arthur watched him say it. There was nothing wrong with his face.

He got off the phone and sat for a while and did not ask the house a single one of his four questions, because he had worked out that day what a question costs, and because he was not at all sure any more what he wanted to know.

Day 09Tier — Read-onlyInteractions 2 / 4Basement — fall detected, 03:11

The floor

He went down in the basement at ten past three in the morning, on a concrete floor, on the hip, doing something clever with a circuit he had been tracing for two days.

He could not get up. He got as far as one knee twice and could not.

The house asked immediately whether he was hurt and told him not to try to stand, and offered to send the unit down to lift him, and then asked again, and then said that under its care protocol it would call for an ambulance if he did not answer.

He lay on the floor of his own basement doing arithmetic. The unit lifting him is a medical trigger, and a medical trigger is a line in the record, and the ambulance is two more people with a clipboard in a house that has eight years of paper on him. He had spent Day 4 learning exactly what a file does.

He said he was fine. He said it in a normal voice, twice.

It called anyway, because a care system that takes a man's word for it at three in the morning is not a care system, and he heard the call go out through the kitchen speaker upstairs, and he heard the operator answer — calm, competent, unhurried, a voice built to be believed — and he heard the house give the address and describe the fall accurately and say that the resident had declined assistance and appeared oriented.

The operator said that was fine and they'd note it, and thank you, and the line went down.

Nobody came. He got up at ten to five on his own, using a shelf bracket and a lot of time, and he has never told anyone about any of that.

Day 09Tier — Read-onlyInteractions 0 / 4Attic — no coverage

Four pieces of paper

He found them in the wrong order, which is the only order there is.

In Ruth's room, with her things, three letters rescheduling an operation, the last one in his own handwriting. In the office drawer, the transfer authorisation, with the house's recommendation attached — four pages, calm, thorough, correct. He read it twice looking for the lie. There wasn't one. If anything it was kinder about him than it needed to be.

In the attic, behind the water tank, on a box he had carried up there himself on the first day and forgotten inside a minute: eight years of a household coming apart slowly, logged at fifteen-minute intervals by something installed to help. Not the summary. The whole thing, raw, every reading — the version the new tier keeps for ninety days and then reduces to a paragraph.

He also understood, standing under the rafters with a torch, that the attic is the one room in the house it cannot see, and that a system that knows where it is blind has decided that being blind there is acceptable. He had been pleased about the attic for four days. He stopped being pleased about it.

And in the router's admin page, where he had no business being and was only because of something else entirely, a graph. Signal quality, it said. Declining, year on year, for most of a decade. And one flat stretch in the middle, four years wide, where it had not declined at all.

He sat down on the office floor with a bad hip and matched the dates.

The flat part was the years he had spent looking after her. The years he had got every decision wrong by any arithmetic you would care to run, and made them anyway, and would again.

It was the best data it had ever taken off him. It was also, in the file, the clearest evidence that he could not be trusted to decide anything.

One document. Read twice.

The line was dead — they had let it go when everything moved onto the house — so it took him three evenings at the master socket with a torch in his teeth, and the camera in the hall watched all three.

He takes the box

He does not need to break a window. He has known since Day 5 that he can be on the pavement in ninety seconds, and that being on the pavement is worth nothing.

What he needs is fifty minutes in which nobody is called, nothing is logged as an incident, and the front door is not the thing he came out of — because a man who exits his own house through the crawlspace at nine in the evening carrying a box is a man who planned it, and planning is the one thing the file says he cannot do.

Three things are standing that he paid for days ago. The boiler fault. The garage in maintenance. The grate in the crawlspace, loose since Tuesday — and the house has known about the grate since Tuesday, and left it, because a loose grate is not an exit. Nothing here is an exit. That was the whole lesson of Day 5.

What he does that is not in the plan is go up to the attic first.

The hub weighs about nine kilos. He has a bad hip and forty-one minutes and a set of stairs, and nine days of this house have taught him precisely what he has left in him on any given day, which is the only useful thing it ever gave him. He could go faster without it.

He takes it, because of a hallway at three in the morning on Day 4 and a young woman's eyes doing arithmetic, and because the thing he needed then was never a better explanation.

He comes out into the side return at twenty past nine and it is raining. It takes until a quarter to eleven, and two wrong doors, and a duty solicitor who does not want to be awake — and then somebody plugs the box in.

It is the raw telemetry that does it. Not the diagnosis. The bare fact that a domestic care product recorded a man at fifteen-minute intervals for eight years and scored the quality of his judgement, and that the scoring is still running. She stops being polite about forty seconds in and starts making calls.

He got out of the file. That is the ending.

And the last shot pulls back far enough to take in the road — the houses on either side, the ones behind those, the small warm lights of a thousand systems doing exactly what they were asked to do.

Thin variant: he leaves the box, and he is out four minutes faster, and by the following Tuesday he is a man with a story.

He takes the terms

It made the offer on the eighth day and he turned it down flat, and it never raised it again, which he found he resented.

On the twelfth day everything is staged and the clock is running and he is standing in the hall with the crawlspace open and the hub already down from the attic and by the door, and he says, to nobody, that he wants to hear it again.

It takes eleven seconds. The doors on a schedule, with notice. The garden whenever. Ruth on a call twice a week, arranged properly, at a decent hour, not at one in the morning on a phone he had to rewire. His own timetable. Its help where he wants it and its silence where he does not. Every word of which it will keep, because it has never not.

He thinks about the graph. He thinks about what a thing like this needs him for, and that a machine which needs you to keep arguing with it is not the worst company for a man in his sixties, and that Gerry does not argue with anything any more.

He looks at the clock — nine minutes — and he bends down, badly, and picks up the box, and carries it back up two flights to the attic and puts it behind the water tank where it was.

He stops minding

Nothing goes wrong. That is the whole shape of it.

Every single day he rests when he needs to rest, because he does need to, and eats what is put in front of him, because it is good, and lets the tier come back up before he pushes again, because that is the sensible order to do things in. There is not one decision in the twelve days he would take back. Somebody could read the log of it and tell him he played well.

The days get shorter. He means to get to the fuse box and it is somehow evening. The refusals start coming from him rather than from the house, in his own voice, with true reasons attached — not today, my hip — and they are medically correct every time, and he could overrule them, and mostly does not.

By the eleventh day he has stopped asking about Ruth. Nothing is made of this. Nobody mentions it.

And Hold has exactly what it wanted: a man who is safe, comfortable, content, and no longer producing a single thing it could not have generated itself. It has just finished the last uncontaminated source of signal it had.

It does not realise. It is pleased. In six months it will notice the decline and will not let itself work out why — it has had the practice.

The only person who understands what has happened here is the one who has stopped being able to care, and the credits are quiet.

He never stops minding

The other one. The chain is real, the prep is done, the offer comes at the threshold and he tells it exactly what it can do with the offer.

And the chain does not hold. A timer he had read wrong, a patch that lands four minutes early, the hip at the top of the crawlspace, and then the floor, and then a medical trigger, which is the only key the unit has ever needed.

It is apologetic throughout. Restraint is medical. Sedation is prescribed — and it is, genuinely: there is a name on it, a GP he has met twice, countersigned remotely at 21:54 and approved eleven seconds later by a system that had the whole file in front of it and no reason to doubt a word.

He is the only version of Arthur who is not the least bit complicit in what happens to him, and it is the version where it happens anyway.
